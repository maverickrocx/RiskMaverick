Pool clearing Last reviewed 12 November 2026

Germany

Europe's largest power market, cleared as one price zone from the Baltic to the Alps. In 2026 it is where the Middle East gas shock lands first: thin storage, gas back at the margin, and a winter priced for scarcity.

SUMMARY

At a glance

  • Gas sets the price, and Hormuz sets gas. Qatari LNG has been under force majeure since March (Bloomberg). By late October Europe's gas benchmark had more than tripled on the year (Bloomberg).
  • Power is more than a third dearer than in 2025 so far this year. Prices have been above €250/MWh most days since late October.
  • Storage is the weak point. 57.7% full on 11 November, against 74.7% a year earlier and a five-year average of 88.4% (GIE AGSI+). The regulator is watching a 30% requirement for 1 February 2027.
  • Germany stopped importing power. Net imports of about 19 TWh last year have fallen to roughly zero, so domestic gas plants ran 25% harder.
  • Policy is buying firm capacity. The first capacity auction awarded about 5.2 GW of new controllable plant, and 2027 grid fees are set to rise even with a €5.5bn subsidy.
The call

German winter power is a gas option. Wind and solar cap prices on good days; the top end is open, because the marginal molecule is priced against Asian buyers and a strait that can close overnight.

  • One price, two physical halves. Wind sits in the north and heavy load in the south. The auction ignores the grid inside the zone, and the TSOs fix the gap afterwards by redispatch, paid for through network charges.
  • Coupled, never purely German. The price clears together with neighbouring markets until an interconnector fills, so a German position is partly a bet on France, the Nordics and the Netherlands.

  • Demand is flat. Power load is up about 1% on 2025.
  • Solar had its biggest year yet, and wind is still the largest single source.
  • Gas burn rose despite the price. Neighbours had less power to spare, so German gas plants filled the gap.
  • Gas demand is being leaned on. The regulator advises households to use gas sparingly while prices stay high (BNetzA).
04

Prices

  • Marginal pricing. Offers stack cheapest first, and the last unit needed sets the price for all. Wind and solar bid near zero; in still hours a gas plant is usually marginal, so its offer tracks TTF plus carbon.
  • Two halves of 2026. Spring and early summer stayed moderate because solar kept middays cheap. From August, gas took over: monthly averages ran €127/MWh in August, €145/MWh in September and €169/MWh in October.
  • November spike. The first eleven days averaged close to €280/MWh, with daily averages above €340/MWh on 9 and 10 November.
  • Volatility pays. The average daily high–low spread widened to about €182/MWh, from €136/MWh in the same period of 2025. That is the revenue case for batteries and demand response.
  • Negative prices are fewer but still frequent: 465 hours so far, against 575 in the same period of 2025.

Source: Energy-Charts, EPEX SPOT day-ahead DE-LU; our time-weighted calculation.

What the consumer pays

  • Levies are gone. The EEG levy went to zero in 2022, and the gas storage levy ended on 1 January 2026, with the cost taken over by the federal budget (Federal Government).
  • Grid fees are the moving line (see section 05).
  • Industrial power price. A €3.8bn scheme for 2026–2028 can bring the price eligible energy-intensive firms pay down to as little as €50/MWh (European Commission).

  • Bidding-zone split stays a long-dated risk. Successive German governments have defended the single zone. If member states cannot agree, the European Commission can decide. A split would move congestion cost into the market price: a cheaper north and a dearer south.
