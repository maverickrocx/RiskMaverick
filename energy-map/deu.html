01

Overview

Germany is the largest power market in Europe and one of the largest buyers of imported gas on the continent. In 2026 both facts matter at once. The Iran war took Qatari LNG out of the market in March, Europe's gas price more than tripled, and Germany entered winter with its gas stores further from full than in any year since the 2022 crisis.

Day-ahead and intraday power clears on EPEX SPOT. Futures, options and emissions allowances trade on EEX. Gas has a single virtual point, Trading Hub Europe (THE). All of it sits inside European market coupling, so a German price is never purely German. It falls out of an auction that clears across most of the continent at the same moment.

The structural story has not changed. Nuclear ended in April 2023, coal is on a legislated exit path, and wind and solar now supply the largest share of generation. What changed in 2026 is the price of the fuel that sets the price in the hours wind and solar cannot cover. QatarEnergy declared force majeure after Iranian strikes in early March and has rolled it forward month by month since (Bloomberg). By late October Europe's gas benchmark had more than tripled on the year to about €97/MWh (Bloomberg).

The result is a market with two faces. On sunny, windy days prices still collapse, and often go negative. In still, dark hours gas-fired plants set the price, and in 2026 that price is set by the global LNG market. Anyone taking a German position is now taking a view on Hormuz.

Why this market matters beyond Germany

Germany is the physical and commercial centre of the continental grid, trading with nine neighbours in both directions, and it holds the largest gas storage capacity in the EU. Its storage level, its gas-fired capacity and its bidding-zone design set the reference conditions for power prices from Denmark to Switzerland.

Market structure

One bidding zone, four transmission system operators, two exchanges, one gas hub. Simple on paper. Strained in practice, and more so in 2026.

One price for a country that is physically two

Germany and Luxembourg form a single bidding zone. Every megawatt-hour clears at the same price, whether it comes from an offshore wind farm in the Baltic or goes into a chemical plant in Bavaria. The auction proceeds as though the network inside the zone had infinite capacity.

It does not. Wind sits in the north and on the coast, the heaviest industrial load in the south and west, and the north–south corridors are not yet big enough to carry the difference. The market produces a schedule the grid cannot physically deliver, and the four TSOs then intervene after the auction to make it work.

Redispatch — the cost of the single zone

That intervention is redispatch. Constrained generators in the north are paid to turn down and plants in the south are paid to turn up. The bill lands in network charges, not in the exchange price. Those charges are now the most politically sensitive line on a German power bill: without federal support, transmission fees for 2027 would more than double (see section 06).

What this means for a position

The exchange price is a national average that ignores location on purpose. The cost of supplying a site is that price plus a grid charge that varies sharply by region. Two identical contracts can have very different economics depending only on where the meter sits.

03

Infrastructure — power and gas

A power grid still waiting for its north–south links, and a gas system rebuilt around Norwegian pipelines and coastal LNG terminals. In 2026 the LNG half of that rebuild is running well below capacity.

Power

Four TSOs

50Hertz in the north and east, TenneT down the centre, Amprion in the west, TransnetBW in the south-west. Separate control areas, one shared bidding zone, joint working on balancing and redispatch.

North–south corridors

High-capacity HVDC links are being built to carry northern wind to southern load. Until they are energised, redispatch stands in for the missing wires.

Nine interconnected neighbours

Denmark, Poland, Czechia, Austria, Switzerland, France, Luxembourg, Belgium and the Netherlands. Germany was a net importer of about 19 TWh between January and mid-November 2025. In the same period of 2026 its cross-border trade was roughly balanced (Energy-Charts).

Gas and LNG

Germany produces very little of its own gas. What defines the system is its entry points: Norwegian pipeline gas, flows from the Netherlands and Belgium, and floating LNG terminals at Wilhelmshaven, Brunsbüttel and Mukran. Pipelines carry almost all of it. In the week to 9 November 2026, 18.1 TWh came in by pipeline and only 1.2 TWh through the LNG terminals, which ran at an average of 29.2% of capacity (Bundesnetzagentur).

That low utilisation is the 2026 story in one number. The terminals are there; the cargoes are not. With roughly a fifth of global LNG supply shut in by the Qatari outage, Asian buyers have outbid Europe for spot cargoes for much of the year (Bloomberg). Qatari loadings had recovered to around half of pre-war levels by early November (Bloomberg).

04

Demand and supply

Flat power demand, record solar, and gas-fired output up sharply despite the price, because Germany stopped leaning on its neighbours.

Demand

German power demand is broadly flat. Load from 1 January to 11 November 2026 was about 401 TWh, around 1% above the same period of 2025 (Energy-Charts). Gas demand is the one to watch. The regulator has advised households to use gas sparingly while prices stay high (Bundesnetzagentur).

Supply

Wind remains the largest single source and solar had its biggest year yet, with output about 21% higher than in the same period of 2025. The surprise is gas. Gas-fired generation rose about 25%, to around 48 TWh, even with gas at crisis prices. The reason is the border: in 2025 Germany imported a large share of its marginal power, while in 2026 its neighbours had less to spare and domestic plants filled the gap.

The refill that never caught up

Germany started the injection season with the emptiest stores since 2022, and the summer curve never paid traders enough to fill them. Stores entered the heating season well below their level in previous years. The regulator still rates the risk of a tight supply as low, but it is now watching one date: a requirement for storage to be 30% full on 1 February 2027.

05

Price setting mechanism

A marginal-cost auction, coupled across Europe. Prices still go negative on sunny days. But in 2026 the hours that matter are set by gas, and gas is set by the Gulf.

How the auction works

Generators and suppliers bid into EPEX SPOT for each quarter-hour of the following day. Offers stack cheapest first, and the last unit needed to meet demand, the marginal unit, sets the price paid to everyone who cleared. Wind and solar bid close to zero. When the weather cooperates they push the expensive end of the stack out of the money. When it does not, a gas-fired plant is usually the marginal unit, and its offer tracks the TTF gas price plus the cost of carbon.

The auction is not run for Germany alone. Under single day-ahead coupling, national order books clear together with whatever cross-border capacity is available, so prices converge until an interconnector fills. A German position is partly a bet on France, the Nordics and the Netherlands.

The year split in two. Prices through spring and early summer were higher than in 2025 but not extreme, because solar kept the middle of the day cheap. From August onwards gas took over. The monthly average rose from €127/MWh in August to €169/MWh in October, and the first eleven days of November averaged close to €280/MWh, with daily averages above €340/MWh on 9 and 10 November.

Volatility is where the return is

The average daily spread between the cheapest and dearest quarter-hour widened by about a third in 2026, to around €180/MWh. The market is paying more than ever for flexibility between hours. That is the arithmetic behind batteries, pumped storage and demand response, and it is why storage developers were disappointed to win nothing in this year's capacity auction (section 06).

What the consumer actually pays

The exchange price is one line on a delivered price. The EEG levy went to zero in 2022 and moved onto the federal budget. The gas storage levy followed it on 1 January 2026, with the cost taken over by the federal government (Federal Government). Network charges are the line still moving. Energy-intensive industry also gets an industrial power price for 2026–2028: a €3.8bn scheme that can bring the price eligible companies pay down to as little as €50/MWh, approved by the European Commission in April 2026 (European Commission).

06

Regulatory regime

A national regulator with broad powers, working inside a European rulebook it does not write. Three files moved in 2026: capacity, grid fees and gas security.

The first capacity auction went to gas

On 3 November 2026 BNetzA published the result of the first auction under the new Electricity Supply Security and Capacity Act (StromVKG). It offered 4,500 MW and received 41 bids for about 11,958 MW. Eleven bids totalling about 5,160 MW won, at a volume-weighted average of €155,296 per MW per year, against a cap of €244,000 (Bundesnetzagentur). North Rhine-Westphalia and Brandenburg took most of the volume, and a second round is being prepared. The plants are new gas-fired capacity to cover the still hours as coal leaves, so each round is a direct signal about Germany's gas demand in the 2030s.

Grid fees: the subsidy shrinks

The federal government is paying €6.5bn towards transmission costs in 2026. For 2027 the cabinet has approved €5.525bn a year through 2029. On that basis the four TSOs set a provisional 2027 transmission fee of 3.54 ct/kWh, up from 2.86 ct/kWh. Without the subsidy it would be 6.52 ct/kWh. The law had not passed when the fees were published, and the TSOs warned that without legal certainty by 7 December 2026 the final fees would rise accordingly (Amprion, for the four TSOs).

Gas security: early warning, not alert

Germany has been at the early warning level of its gas emergency plan since 1 July 2025, when the alert level in place since June 2022 was lifted. Despite the low storage, BNetzA's assessment of 11 November says supply is stable and the risk of a tight supply remains low, while it monitors the 30% storage requirement for 1 February 2027 (Bundesnetzagentur). Escalation to the alert or emergency level would be a ministry decision, and at the emergency level BNetzA would take over gas allocation.

Coal and the single zone

The coal exit is running ahead of schedule. In September BNetzA said plants that had already left the market exceeded the legal target for 2029, so no forced closures were needed this year (Bundesnetzagentur). Plants the grid still needs can be held in reserve. The bidding-zone question has not gone away either. Successive German governments have defended the single German–Luxembourg zone. If member states cannot agree, the European Commission can decide. Splitting the zone would move congestion cost from network charges into the market price, creating a cheaper north and a dearer south. It belongs in the risk column for any long-dated German position.

Winter 2026/27 — what to watch

The winter will be decided by weather, Hormuz and Asian demand, in roughly that order.

The risk view

German winter power is now a gas option. The cheap end of the curve is protected by solar and wind, which cap prices on good days. The expensive end is open, because the marginal molecule is priced against Asian buyers and a strait that can close overnight. Long-volatility positions have been paid in 2026; short ones should be sized for a cold February.
